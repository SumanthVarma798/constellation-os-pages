Investor brief · September 2026 · Hyderabad

Software that makes a fleet of satellites earn its keep.

Constellation OS plans the day for every satellite and every ground station a company runs, prices each decision in rupees, and repairs the plan when reality breaks it. It sits above the control systems operators already own. It never touches spacecraft safety.

The two-minute version. A satellite in orbit only earns money when it is pointed at the right place at the right time and its data is brought back down through a ground station rented by the minute. Past about ten satellites, doing that by hand — calendars, spreadsheets, scripts, round-the-clock shifts — stops working. India is now funding real fleets. Nobody domestic sells the planning layer they will need. The core is built, independently reviewed and running against a real mission-control system; what it needs now is a first design partner and the capital to get there.

Part 1 of 3

The problem, and what the industry is doing about it

Launch got cheap. Operations did not. The bottleneck moved from getting to orbit to using orbit well.

What a satellite operator actually has to decide, every day

An Earth-observation satellite circles the planet every ~95 minutes. It can only photograph a target while it is overhead, only talk to a ground station while that station is in view (a few minutes per pass), and only hold so much data and battery in between. A company with twenty satellites, eight ground stations and three hundred customer orders faces tens of thousands of possible “windows” a week, most of which conflict with each other.

The plan has to answer: which order gets which satellite, which pass downlinks it, which pass carries the commands up, whether the battery and storage survive the sequence, and whether the customer’s deadline is met. Then a station goes offline, or a satellite enters safe mode, and the whole thing has to be re-decided in minutes without disturbing what has already been sent to the spacecraft.

Every ground-station minute costs real money — the commercial floor is roughly $3/min — so a failed or wasted pass is a line item, not an inconvenience.

Why it gets hard past ~10 satellites

Conflicts grow with satellites × stations × orders × constraints. A person can hold a 3-satellite week in their head. A 20-satellite week is a combinatorial optimisation problem, and greedy “first come, first served” schedules leave measurable value on the table. In the academic literature, two-step optimisation beats greedy scheduling by about 35%.

What industry experts are doing about it

Four responses exist. Each solves part of the problem for a different kind of buyer.

Who
Pixxel, Dhruva Space, GalaxEye in India; Planet, Spire and most large operators globally.
How
Hire ground-software engineers to write monitoring, tasking and scheduling tools; run a 24/7 shift-based mission control; stitch external ground networks (KSAT, AWS Ground Station) in with scripts.
Works when
The fleet is small, the team is close to the spacecraft, and the ops problem is mostly “keep the lights on”.
Breaks when
The fleet doubles. Priority rules become tribal knowledge, planning and downlink are optimised in different tools, and a feasible image plan turns out impossible to downlink on time. The US market looked exactly like this around 2019 before Quindar, Epsilon3 and Cognitive Space were funded.
Who
Kratos OpenSpace, GMV Flexplan, Terma PLAN, L3Harris, Airbus ground segments.
How
Configurable mission-planning suites with rule engines and genetic-algorithm schedulers, sold into agencies and defence primes as multi-year programmes.
Works when
The buyer is a government programme with a decade-long timeline and a procurement office.
Breaks when
The buyer is a 40-person start-up with a fleet arriving next year. Quindar’s own pitch: legacy ground systems take “more than a decade to field”. Price and integration time exclude the operators India is now producing.
Who
Quindar (US, ~$27M, Series A Nov 2025), Cognitive Space (US, ~$11M), Leanspace (France, €10M Series A Nov 2025, 20+ operators), Saber Astronautics (Australia/US, Saber Commander launched July 2026), Antaris (US/Chennai).
How
Cloud mission-management SaaS that sits on an open-source C2 engine (Quindar runs on OpenC3 COSMOS) and orchestrates scheduling, ground-network booking and automation above it. This is the market-validated shape of the category.
Works when
The customer is a US or European commercial operator comfortable with a foreign cloud control plane.
Breaks when
The work is defence-adjacent in India. Ownership and data-residency rules keep foreign SaaS out of SBS-3-class programmes, and none of these companies has an Indian presence. Cautionary tale: Major Tom, the earliest cloud mission-control product, ran ~a dozen satellites after eight years and was absorbed by a small operator in 2022. Standalone “cloud mission control” at small scale is a hard business; the wedge has to be measurable money.
Who
Yamcs (Space Applications Services), OpenC3 COSMOS, NASA OpenMCT, SatNOGS, Orekit, TAT-C.
How
Free, flight-proven command-and-control and visualisation engines. Operators run them and write the planning layer themselves.
Works when
You need telemetry and commanding — that layer is genuinely solved by open source and should never be rebuilt.
Breaks when
You need a plan. The maintainers say so themselves: OpenC3’s calendar is “typically used with a separately developed integration to full fledged mission planning software”; Yamcs users asking for pass scheduling were pointed to external scripts. SatNOGS operators maintain hand-edited priority files driven by cron. Planning lives outside the engine, in whatever the team improvised.

The gap in one sentence

Command-and-control is solved. Flight dynamics is solved. The layer that turns customer orders into a conflict-free, money-aware plan across satellites and stations — and learns from what actually executed — is either a decade-old defence product, a foreign cloud SaaS, or a spreadsheet. In India it is a spreadsheet.

Part 2 of 3

The product: what it does, what it is worth, what is under the hood

A money-first planning and mission-operations layer that sits above existing command-and-control. It owns the operator’s workflow end to end and leaves spacecraft safety exactly where it is.

The workflow it owns — click through it

The value proposition, in the customer’s units

More delivered value per rupee of ground-segment spend. The optimiser’s objective is literally rupees: customer value delivered on time, minus station minutes at the station’s per-minute rate, minus lateness penalties. Every plan states its exact value and every refused order carries a typed reason (“no downlink capacity before deadline”, “would breach storage”, “displaced by a higher-value order”). An operations lead can read a plan the way a finance lead reads a budget.

A proven bound, not a guess. Most schedulers return “a good plan”. This one returns a plan plus a solver-proven certificate that no plan exists worth more than 10% above it. That is the difference between a heuristic and an answer — and it is what lets an operator approve quickly.

Repair without rewriting. When a station drops or an urgent order lands, the re-solve starts from what has already executed, is penalised for moving commitments, and reports in money: “preserved ₹X of committed value, cost ₹Y of extra station time, moved N activities (listed)”.

Nothing to rip out. It integrates with the C2 and flight-dynamics tools the operator already built or bought. Self-hostable, on-premises, auditable — the shape that defence-adjacent Indian buyers can actually accept.

Who buys, and why each cares

Data operators (Pixxel, GalaxEye, PierSight) sell pixels against customer clocks — they buy on-time delivery and unallocated passes turned into revenue.

Operations providers (Dhruva) sell contact minutes to other operators — they buy utilisation and explainable displacement.

Sovereign programmes (SBS-3, EO-PPP) answer to a mandate — they buy auditability, on-prem deployment and a domestic vendor.

The fixed safety boundary

Only the operator-approved, allow-listed plan reaches the control system, and only through a separate worker. No browser, no AI model, and no natural-language output can ever issue a spacecraft command or touch fault-protection settings. Flight software keeps that authority, permanently and by design. The public claim is never “AI runs satellites”, because it doesn’t.

How it is built

Every feature is one written spec, one issue, one PR, with an independent review of the spec and a second independent review of the implementation before merge. Everything is reproducible from a clean checkout — the same inputs give byte-identical plans. Four claim classes stay separate and are never collapsed: repeatability (proven by hashes), protocol correctness (proven against stock Yamcs), operational robustness (proven by scripted disruptions), and physical-flight reliability (not yet proven — it needs a real spacecraft).

The pattern we are following

US operators built everything in-house until fleets scaled past ~10 satellites around 2019. Then Quindar, Epsilon3 and Cognitive Space were founded and funded. India in 2026 looks like the US in 2019: in-house is the incumbent, the fleets are about to double, and the category has no domestic entrant.

The moat

Ownership and data-residency rules keep foreign SaaS out of defence-adjacent Indian work. A self-hosted, explainable, domestic product is defensible in a way a US cloud competitor’s is not — and the same product sells to small operators worldwide that Quindar-class pricing leaves behind.

The honest risk

Government timelines slip; treat that as the base case, not the exception. And the concentrated-buyer structure below is real.

Business model

Annual software licence, self-hosted, priced per satellite under management — the unit the customer already counts in, and one that grows with every launch they make. A one-time integration fee covers connecting to the operator’s C2, ground network and tasking tools. Upsell is by capability: repair, economics, the machine tasking API, then the multi-operator layer.

Why per satellite. Ground-station time is already sold by the minute, so the customer can compute the return themselves: one avoided wasted pass a week pays for a satellite’s licence. The product’s own economics page shows planned-versus-realised rupees, which becomes the renewal conversation.

What is not yet known. The price point. No public number exists for this layer; the first operator conversations run a structured price-sensitivity study before any contract is quoted. Until then every revenue figure on this page inherits that uncertainty and says so.

Revenue shape, years 1–3

Grants and one to two design-partner contracts carry years one and two; IN-SPACe Seed (≤ ₹1 cr) and the Technology Adoption Fund (≤ ₹25 cr, up to 60% of project cost) exist precisely for this stage. The venture-scale story is the multi-operator phase as the EO-PPP and SBS-3 fleets ramp in 2027–29.

Why it sticks

Once the planner holds an operator’s constraints, priorities and execution history, it is the system of record for what the fleet did and why. Switching means re-teaching a year of operational knowledge. Combined with on-prem deployment inside a defence-adjacent programme, churn is a procurement event, not a click.

What could kill it, and what we do about each

Government timelines slip
Base case, not exception. Mitigation: sell to the commercial data operators (Pixxel, GalaxEye, KaleidEO) whose clocks are set by paying customers, and to small global operators, so the sovereign programmes are upside rather than the plan.
Operators keep building in-house
The cheap kill, by design. We are not asking anyone to replace their stack — we integrate above it. If a fleet of 20 still prefers a spreadsheet after seeing a proven, explained, money-denominated plan run against their own Yamcs, the company stops having cost a webpage and a year, not a funding round.
Two or three buyers control the Indian market
True. Countered by high-value, sticky contracts once embedded, by selling the same product to the global small-operator segment from India, and by treating Dhruva as a channel as much as a competitor.
Quindar or Leanspace enters India through a partner
Possible. Their answer is still a foreign cloud control plane, which defence-adjacent buyers cannot accept. Our answer is on-prem, auditable and domestic. The best defence is a working product in an Indian operator’s hands before they arrive.
Solo founder
Real. Named gaps are a co-founder and a first design partner. The build has been run under an independent-review process from day one precisely so the codebase does not depend on one person’s memory. There is a hard checkpoint on 15 January 2027: design-partner traction by then, or the product becomes the credential for an insider path inside an operator.
