A first-time founder's most common mistake at this stage is to keep building because building feels like progress and selling feels like rejection. You have shipped enough to be credible. The next 90 days are a sales job, and the code should change only when a real operator asks for something in a real meeting.

The three clocks you are racing

Every timing decision — when to sell, when to raise, when to hire — is a question of which clock runs out first.

— monthsRunway clock. Savings ÷ monthly burn. Yours is long for a solo founder, which is the single biggest advantage you have. Do not shorten it with hires before a customer exists.
9–18 monthsSales-cycle clock. A first enterprise contract in space takes 9–18 months from first meeting to signature. A conversation started today closes in mid-to-late 2027. This is why customers come before everything.
2027–29Fleet-ramp clock. EO-PPP and SBS-3 satellites launch in this window. The planning pain arrives with them. Be installed beside a fleet before it doubles, not after.

How they combine

Your runway outlasts the sales cycle, so you can afford to sell properly and raise later on evidence rather than on hope. The trap is the opposite: spending eight months polishing, then discovering the sales cycle starts at zero with ten months of savings left. Start the sales clock this week; it is the only one you cannot buy back.

Reading the numbers. On the base case — a modest Hyderabad life, no salary, cloud and tooling under ₹30,000 a month — your savings carry you well past the January checkpoint and through the whole of a 9–18-month sales cycle. That is unusual, and it is why the advice here is "sell first, raise on evidence" rather than "raise now".

The two rules the amber and red bands encode. Investors read runway as posture. Nine months left is a founder with options; three months left is a founder who will take any terms, and they price accordingly. Open a pre-seed process while the green band is still long. Never let the red band arrive without a signed round or a decision to take the insider path.

What is not in this calculator. Health insurance, a family obligation, a wedding, a visa. Add them to personal burn honestly; a runway you flinch at is not a runway.

A grant changes the shape, not the rule

An IN-SPACe Seed grant (≤ ₹1 crore, paid in milestone tranches) can extend runway by a year. Enter it above only once it is approved — pipeline money is not runway.

The "with two hires" scenario is a warning

Two engineers at Hyderabad rates roughly triples burn and cuts runway to under a year. That is what a pre-seed round is for. Do not hire on savings.

4

Customers first — starting this week

When should you reach out? You are already late by the sales-cycle clock, and exactly on time by the product clock. Both say now.

Who, in order. KaleidEO first — a hybrid build-plus-buy stack with mission planning under construction, the softest door in the evidence base. Then GalaxEye (staffing a shift-based ops centre now), then Pixxel (in-house incumbent, fleet doubling), then Dhruva (buyer, channel and competitor at once — go in knowing that). PierSight when you know what they run. In parallel, three to five small operators abroad with 5–30 satellites: the segment Quindar-class pricing leaves behind.

How to ask for the meeting. Not "can I show you my product". Say what you found: "I mined the public issue trackers and job postings and it looks like planning still lives in scripts and priority files outside the C2 — is that true for you, and what breaks when the fleet doubles?" You are offering them a finding about their own industry, and asking them to correct it. People take that meeting.

What a meeting is for. Learning how they plan today, what a missed pass costs them, who decides displacement, and whether a self-hosted planner beside their C2 is something they could ever run. Not pitching. If they ask to see the plan, show it; if they don't, don't.

The design-partner offer, when they lean in. One page. Self-hosted install beside their C2; adapters to their ground network and tasking tools; a 45-minute call every two weeks and async feedback within five days; preferential pricing locked for two years; a hard end date with a binary ask — go paid or stop. Three to five partners, no more. Ask for an LOI when they say "when can we start", not before.

Eight questions that do the work

  1. Who are your customers, and which would notice a collection a day late?
  2. How long from a customer request to a committed window — and is a person in that path?
  3. What fraction of your passes go unallocated in a normal week?
  4. When an urgent request lands, who decides what is displaced, and can you explain it to the customer who lost?
  5. Do you have customers who are contractually never displaced? How is that enforced?
  6. Has anyone ever paid extra to jump the queue? How did you price it?
  7. Can a customer task you through an API with no human at your end?
  8. What happens to your planning process when the fleet doubles?

Never say

Any fixture-derived rupee figure as if it were theirs. That dynamic replanning is a validated need (it is still a hypothesis). "AI runs your satellites." Anything about Dhruva's stack you learned from a job posting as if you had seen it.

Weekly quota

Five new outreach messages, two conversations, one written debrief per conversation into founder-ops. Thirty qualified conversations by the checkpoint is a triumph; ten is enough to know.

Why raise, and when — bootstrap versus venture money

Why anyone raises. Because the milestone that creates value cannot be reached with the cash on hand before a clock runs out. For you the milestone is a deployed design partner with contracted revenue, and the clock is the 2027–29 fleet ramp. Grants and savings can get you to the LOI. They cannot pay two engineers and an operations co-founder for eighteen months while an operator's procurement grinds.

Why not now. A solo, pre-revenue technical founder with no LOI raises slowly, at poor terms, or not at all — and spends the four months the process takes not selling. The same founder with a working product, a signed LOI and a deployment date raises in weeks. The product is built; the LOI is the thing that changes the conversation. Get it first.

The bootstrap case, taken seriously. If a design partner pays even ₹25–40 lakh a year and a grant lands, you can run lean to a second customer without dilution. The venture case wins only if speed matters — and here it does, because the sovereign fleets arrive on a fixed calendar and a foreign competitor could enter through a partner. So: bootstrap through the LOI, then raise a modest pre-seed to move fast, then let contracted revenue set the seed round's price.

The hybrid that fits you. Non-dilutive money first and in parallel (it takes months and does not wait), savings for living, a pre-seed opened only with an LOI in hand and nine-plus months of runway still on the clock.

Reference points, 2026: global pre-seed $500K–$2.5M, median ≈ $1.2M; Indian deep-tech pre-seed commonly ₹2–6 crore; typical dilution 10–20% for the round in total. Instruments: SAFE or iSAFE for angels, CCPS for institutional. Never give one early investor more than 20%.

What the round buys, in the investor's language. "Eighteen months to a deployed design partner with contracted revenue and a second operator in pipeline, with an operations co-founder and two engineers, timed to the 2027–29 fleet ramp." One sentence, one milestone, one clock.

What it must not buy. Features nobody asked for, a bigger office, a conference calendar. If a cost does not move the milestone, it waits for the seed.

Salary is not greed

Pay yourself a modest salary from the round. A founder living on fumes makes fearful decisions, and investors know it; a small salary line is read as maturity.

What investors actually read

3 min 44 sAverage time a VC spends on a seed deck (DocSend, 2026). Pre-seed: about four minutes. Only 58% reach the last slide.
Team, then financialsThe two slides that get the most time — not product. Investors can judge a product fast; they are betting on you and on whether you understand money.
First 4 slides = 60%Of total attention. Problem, why now, solution and proof must carry the argument; everything after is evidence.

Deep-tech and space specifics. Separate technical risk from market risk and show which one the round retires. Map the raise to a milestone, not a runway. Show non-dilutive money pursued in parallel — it signals capital efficiency. Name the safety boundary before they ask about "AI running satellites".

Your deck already does this. Thirteen slides, evidence in the notes, every number labelled. What it lacks is the one thing only the world can supply: a named operator saying yes. Until then, present it as "this is what we'll show you the day the LOI is signed", and mean it.

Founder hygiene — the things first-timers skip

Legal and money setup, this month
Private limited company (not LLP — investors and grants expect it). DPIIT start-up recognition (needed for IN-SPACe Seed and SISFS). All code and IP assigned to the company by a signed deed on day one. A separate business bank account; never mix savings and company money after incorporation. A chartered accountant who has done start-up compliance. Reserve a 10–15% ESOP pool at the first priced round, not before.
The co-founder you actually need
Not another backend engineer. Someone who has run a mission-operations centre or sold into one — Dhruva, ISRO/ISTRAC alumni, KSAT or Leaf Space veterans, a former operator at Pixxel or GalaxEye. Where to look: T-Hub ORBIT cohorts, IN-SPACe investor days, the people who reply to your outreach and say "I wish I had this when I ran ops". Vesting: four years, one-year cliff, for both of you, from the day they join.
Mentors, and what to ask them for
Three is enough: one who has operated a fleet, one who has sold to Indian space or defence procurement, one who has built and sold a B2B SaaS company. Ask for a 30-minute call a month and one introduction a quarter. Send them a one-paragraph update on the first of every month whether or not anything happened; consistency is what turns a mentor into an investor or a reference.
Solo-founder risk, managed rather than denied
You have already written the anti-relapse document; keep using it — doubts get checked against it, not re-litigated daily. Take one full day off a week; the sales clock does not tick faster if you burn out. Health insurance is a runway cost, not a luxury. Tell two people the real numbers so somebody can call you out.
Public claims discipline
Never "AI autonomously runs satellites". Never a fixture rupee figure presented as an operator's. Never "published paper" for work that was submitted but not published. Never Dhruva's stack described as if you had seen it. The company's credibility is the founder's credibility, and in a market of eight to twelve accounts, everybody talks to everybody.

12

The checkpoint, and what each outcome means

LOI signed by 15 Jan 2027

Continue exactly as above. Deploy, file grants, open the pre-seed on evidence. The insider path is closed on purpose, and that is fine — you chose speed and identity.

No LOI by 15 Jan 2027

Take the operator role you have already identified, with the product as the evidence of field experience, and run the insider path the Quindar founders ran. The code stays yours; the market does not disappear; the checkpoint converts stubbornness into strategy. Reopen only with a new signal — a warm inbound, a grant approved, a co-founder with a fleet behind them.

What "traction signal" means, precisely, so that January cannot be argued with: a signed letter of intent from an operator naming a start date, or a paid pilot of any size, or a grant approval plus two operators in active design-partner negotiation. Anything less is interest, and interest is not a reason to keep spending savings.
